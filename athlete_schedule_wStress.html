What the full period shows (Jul 5 – Aug 8)

This window covers a stay-away swim camp and what followed it: ① arrival and settling in, ② a facility found and regular training resumed, ③ a shift to studying for an exam with minimal exercise, ④ Dad's visit, ⑤ the flight home, ⑥ the test itself, ⑦ a 4-day trip to Nantucket, MA with friends, ⑧ an early rise to watch the sunrise, and ⑨ the trip home. Markers ⑥–⑨ (Jul 25 onward) fall outside the Jul 5–24 baseline window and are scored against it, not folded into it — see the callout above. That context changes how the stress index should be read throughout — it was built to track training load, and training wasn't the only thing happening.

  • The stress index weighs sleep architecture — deep-sleep share of total sleep and time spent awake during the sleep session — not just total hours and HRV/RHR. Several days' rankings changed enough to matter once it did; the clearest are called out below.
  • Days 1–5 (Jul 5–9), before the facility was found: no workouts logged, yet the stress index skews positive (up to +0.66 on Jul 7, where deep sleep was just 8% of total — the second-lowest of the baseline) and sleep data is missing on two of those five days. With no training load to explain it, this reads as adjustment stress — a new environment and a disrupted routine — not overtraining.
  • Jul 10 onward, once a facility was located: the brick-training block (Jul 13–19: swim + bike + strength + walk on the same day, peaking at 1,388 kcal on Jul 14) shows the pattern you want from productive training — stress rises on hard days and clears within 1–2 days (Jul 15 hits one of the baseline's lowest points right after the heaviest stretch). VO2max climbs almost the whole way through the baseline, 48.9 → 50.8 ml/(kg·min) (+3.8%): the load was well tolerated.
  • Jul 22 is the single highest-stress day in the baseline (+0.84), and sleep architecture is why it reads that high. HRV craters to 31.4 ms — the lowest baseline reading — on a day with almost no exercise (two short walks). That night's sleep also had the joint-highest fragmentation of the baseline (0.83h awake). Two independent channels — autonomic and sleep quality — both moved on the day exam prep began, a stronger signal than either alone.
  • Sleep architecture also splits Jul 23 and Jul 24 apart, which duration alone couldn't do. Jul 24 has the best deep-sleep share of the baseline (27.8%, next-highest is 21.8%) and is the second-most-recovered baseline day (−0.78). Jul 23 has excellent RHR (57 bpm, tied lowest) but below-average deep sleep (13.9%) and above-average fragmentation (0.46h) — a more middling −0.46 once sleep quality, not just calm RHR, is counted. Body calm, sleep not actually that restorative that particular night.
  • HRV and RHR barely track each other across the baseline window (r ≈ 0.00) — a single "recovery" number would have blurred the training-block oscillation, the adjustment-week elevation, and the Jul 22 spike into one flat average. Reading the trend day-by-day, with context, is what surfaced all three.
  • Combining the two Health Auto Export CSVs corrected a partial-day artifact: the original export's Jul 26 row (generated before that day fully synced) showed 43.83 kcal; the newer export's Jul 26 row shows 912.74 kcal for the same date. The more recently generated file wins on any overlapping date for exactly this reason.
  • Training mix shifted toward swimming and strength work after the baseline — Pool Swim (1,325 kcal, 5 sessions) and Functional Strength (1,358 kcal, 3 sessions) together account for ~70% of the post-baseline period's 3,817 workout kcal, while Outdoor Cycling nearly disappeared (558 kcal across just 2 sessions, vs. 669 kcal across 16 sessions in the baseline). Outdoor Walk stayed the most frequent activity (8 sessions) but the smallest kcal contributor (576 kcal).
  • Jul 27–30 was the 4-day Nantucket trip (left home the morning of the 27th, home the afternoon of the 30th) — no structured workouts during the trip; the four Outdoor Cycling/Walking sessions logged Jul 28 (~700 kcal) read as casual biking and walking around the island, part of the training-mix shift above rather than real training days.
  • Jul 29's spike to the single highest-stress reading of the whole period (+1.90) has a clean explanation once the trip context is added: just 1.52h of logged sleep that night, from rising early with friends to watch the sunrise — a genuinely short night, not a sleep-tracking gap.
  • The frozen baseline itself is untouched by any of this — AH_stress_baseline.json's Jul 5–24 mean/std are exactly what they were before the extension; every day from Jul 25 on is measured against that fixed reference, not re-centering the average as new data arrives.
Caveats
  • Weight, blood pressure, SpO₂ and body composition are entirely unlogged in this export — the columns exist but every value is blank, so they're excluded here.
  • Sleep and respiratory rate are missing on Jul 5 and Jul 8 (baseline) and Jul 28 (post-baseline, likely the Watch off the wrist at some point during the Nantucket trip) — the stress index for those three days leans more on HRV/RHR alone.
  • Deep-sleep share is a night-to-night ratio (deep hours ÷ total asleep hours), not a validated sleep-quality score — the export's "Asleep" and "In Bed" fields aren't populated by the Watch (always 0, except two early nights that fall back to an unstaged total), so this is the most detail the data supports; bedtime/wake-time consistency isn't available at all in this daily-aggregate export.
  • The nine numbered markers are read from `AH_events.json`, a small dated log kept alongside the data — not inferred from the biometrics. Without that context, Jul 5–9, Jul 22, and Jul 29 would likely have been misread as unexplained noise, detraining, or a data gap.
  • Coverage is uneven even with nine markers logged — most days in this period, in both the baseline and after it, have no logged context at all. A day that reads as elevated or calm with no nearby marker may just be undocumented, not uneventful.
  • Jul 25's HRV and RHR are Apple Watch's overnight/early-morning readings, i.e. they mostly reflect the Jul 24→25 night and waking state at home, not a real-time reading captured during the test itself — treat the +0.18 score as "how keyed-up his body was heading into test day," not a live measurement of the exam.
  • VO2max readings are sparser after the baseline — only 6 of the 15 post-baseline days have a reading, on top of already-sparse baseline coverage — treat the 50.98 → 49.88 ml/(kg·min) change as a rough signal, not a precise trend line.
  • The composite stress index is a simple unweighted z-score blend for this analysis, not a validated clinical or Whoop/Oura-style algorithm, and it can't distinguish physical from psychological load on its own — treat it as directional, not diagnostic.
